How these tests work

Correct nulls, not textbook ones

Games that draw several numbers at once do not produce independent counts — the balls in one draw are mutually exclusive. Frequency tests therefore compare against a Monte-Carlo null that simulates real without-replacement draws. Carry-over tests use a shuffled-order null, because overlaps at a given lag share draws with their neighbours.

Pools calibrated from the data

Number pools are read off the archive rather than assumed, and the history is split wherever the pool was re-sized. Assuming a wider pool than the game actually used invents balls that can never be drawn and produces a spectacular but meaningless result.

Multiple testing

At a 5% threshold roughly {{ (0.05 * (tested|length)) | round | int }} of these {{ tested|length }} tests would look significant on perfectly fair data. The q column controls the expected proportion of false discoveries among the flagged results.

What a flag is not

It is not evidence of wrongdoing. Transcription errors on the source sites, rule changes and equipment changes all produce departures from the idealised model, and in this archive they are by far the most common cause.
